## OVERVIEW

This film takes a curious beginner on a sixty-minute journey through the interlocking economic and geopolitical crises that converged in March 2026. We begin with something concrete: an ordinary gas pump — the place where abstract macroeconomics becomes viscerally personal. From there, the film builds layer by layer, introducing each crisis not as an isolated headline but as a thread in a web of cause and consequence. The viewer learns that a missile strike on a Qatari gas terminal and a 40-trillion-dollar national debt and a Bitcoin crash from $126,000 to $60,000 are not separate stories — they are the same story told from different vantage points. The film's emotional arc moves from bewildered anxiety (why does everything feel broken at once?) through careful understanding of each mechanism, to a synthesis moment where the beginner can finally see the whole architecture: a monetary system that has been quietly accumulating structural stress for decades, now meeting a cluster of external shocks it was never designed to absorb. Two visual worlds anchor the film throughout. The first is the **trading floor at the open** — the physical theater of market panic, where human beings respond to abstract forces with visceral speed. The second is the **family kitchen table** — the intimate, domestic counterpoint where the same forces arrive not as price charts but as grocery bills, mortgage payments, and kitchen-table conversations about whether to take a second job. These two worlds alternate and eventually merge, revealing that high finance and household survival are the same crisis viewed from opposite ends of a telescope.

### SCENE 1: THE PUMP
**Duration:** 85 seconds | **Phase:** Wonder | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands that abstract economic forces arrive in lived experience first at the gas pump — a physical threshold between the macro world and the personal world

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "March 2026. You pull into a gas station somewhere in America or Europe or anywhere that runs on oil — which is basically everywhere — and you look at the price on the pump. And something in your stomach drops. Not because you didn't expect it, but because you expected it to have gotten better by now. So you find yourself standing there wondering: how did we get here? And why does it feel like everything is going wrong at the same time?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "That feeling — that something is going wrong in multiple places at once — is not anxiety. It is correct observation. In March 2026, economists gave it a name borrowed from complexity theory: a polycrisis. A polycrisis is not many crises happening simultaneously by coincidence. It is many crises that are feeding one another, amplifying one another, and making each other harder to solve. The war in the Middle East raises oil prices, which raises inflation, which constrains the Federal Reserve, which pressures the bond market, which stresses the banks, which tightens credit, which slows growth, which weakens consumer spending, which reduces tax revenue, which expands the deficit, which pushes up bond yields, which raises mortgage rates, which freezes the housing market, which shrinks household wealth, which — eventually — sends that feeling of dread straight through you and out the other side as a number on a gas pump."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "Over the next hour, we are going to trace every one of those connections. By the end, the gas pump will not feel random or overwhelming. It will feel like exactly what it is: the outermost ring of a system you can understand. Let's start at the beginning."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The camera opens at extreme macro on a gas pump nozzle, held in a human hand — knuckles slightly tense, cold light of a gray morning catching the chrome edge of the nozzle. The depth of field is very shallow; the display price beyond is a warm amber blur. The 0.5 px/frame micro-drift is imperceptible but constant. As V1 speaks, the camera breathes very slowly back, bringing the display price gently into focus — but we never see the actual number. What we see is the hand's grip tightening almost imperceptibly. The light is early morning: blue-gray sky reflected in the stainless steel surfaces of the pump. As V2 begins the chain description, the camera does something important: it begins an extremely slow backward pull, frame widening from the nozzle to the hand, to the car, to the forecourt, to the street, to the city behind it — each phrase in V2's chain corresponding to a small further recession of the lens. By the time V2 finishes, we are looking at an ordinary urban street from a moderate distance, the gas station just one small business among many. At V3's words, a warm dissolve begins — the blue-gray morning light softens toward gold.

### SCENE 2: A BRIEF HISTORY OF EASY MONEY
**Duration:** 120 seconds | **Phase:** Wonder → Curiosity | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands that the 2008 financial crisis was "solved" by creating money at an unprecedented scale — and that this solution deferred rather than eliminated the underlying problem

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "But hang on — I keep hearing that the Federal Reserve can just print money when things get bad. So if that's true, why doesn't the Fed just print its way out of this? Didn't they do that in 2008?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "They did. And that is precisely the problem. Here is the most important piece of financial history a beginner can know. In 2008, the American banking system was on the edge of collapse because it had lent enormous amounts of money against assets — specifically, home mortgages — that turned out to be worth far less than the loans written against them. The Federal Reserve's response was something entirely new in modern American history: it began buying those impaired assets directly, paying for them with money it created out of nothing. This is called quantitative easing, or QE. The Fed's balance sheet — essentially the running total of everything it has purchased using created money — grew from under one trillion dollars in 2008 to over four and a half trillion dollars by 2015. It was the largest peacetime monetary expansion in history. And it worked, in the narrow sense that the banking system did not collapse. But here is what QE also did. It flooded the financial system with new money that had to go somewhere. And that money flowed overwhelmingly into assets: stocks, bonds, real estate, eventually Bitcoin and art and anything else that could be owned. Asset prices rose dramatically. The people who owned assets — who were, by definition, already wealthy — became dramatically wealthier. The people who did not own assets — who were, by definition, already less wealthy — did not benefit at all from this flood of new money. Kevin Warsh, who just became the new Fed Chair in early 2026, had been saying this explicitly for fifteen years. He called it a direct cause of widening wealth inequality. And he is right. But here is the quiet, dangerous truth that follows from that observation: the monetary system now depends on this process continuing. The moment it stops — the moment the government can no longer borrow cheaply and spend freely — the entire economy enters what economists call a deflationary death spiral. Which means the very person appointed to stop the money printing is structurally constrained to keep it going."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "This is the essential knot at the center of the whole crisis. The cure for the last problem became the soil in which today's problems grow. Hold that in mind — it will come back in almost every scene we visit."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The kitchen table world takes over for the first time. We are looking at a round wooden kitchen table, morning light slanting through a window from the left. On the table: a coffee mug, a newspaper (blurred, unreadable, just texture), and a phone face-up with a price chart visible but not readable. The camera starts with a medium close-up of the coffee mug and slowly, in the micro-drift rhythm, explores the table surface. When V2 says "quantitative easing," the camera finds a physical object that has been placed at the table edge: a simple mason jar half-full of water. When V2 describes money flowing into assets, a second hand enters frame and gently tips a second jar of tinted water into the first — the water level rises visibly. When the text arrives at "asset prices rose dramatically," a shaft of morning sunlight catches the water surface and it glows gold. When V2 says "the people who did not own assets did not benefit," the second jar — now empty — is set down in shadow, out of the warm light. The camera holds on the contrast: the full, glowing jar in light, the empty jar in shadow. For the final section about the deflationary death spiral, the camera slowly zooms toward the surface of the full jar — the water now very still, but with a barely perceptible surface tension, as if waiting.

### SCENE 3: THE FED'S IMPOSSIBLE CORNER
**Duration:** 90 seconds | **Phase:** Curiosity | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific structural trap the Federal Reserve faces — and why it cannot simply raise rates to fight inflation without creating a worse crisis

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "Okay, so the Fed has been printing money for years and that caused inflation. So why doesn't it just raise interest rates to fight the inflation? Isn't that what it's supposed to do?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "In a normal economy with a manageable debt level, yes, that is exactly what the Fed would do — and it is what it did in 2022 and 2023. Rates went from essentially zero up to 5.25%, the fastest rate hike cycle in forty years. And it did slow inflation, at first. But here is the problem the Fed has created for itself over those fifteen years of easy money. The US government now carries roughly forty trillion dollars in national debt. Every single percentage point increase in interest rates adds hundreds of billions of dollars in annual interest payments to that debt. At 5%, the government is already paying over a trillion dollars a year just in interest — more than it spends on defense, more than it spends on Medicare. If the Fed were to raise rates to the 21% level Paul Volcker used in 1981 to finally break the inflation of the 1970s, interest payments would consume more than all federal tax revenue. The government would be bankrupt before the cure worked. So the Fed is in what economists call a corner. Raising rates kills the debt-financing machinery. Cutting rates or printing more money feeds the inflation. There is no comfortable middle path. Every choice makes something worse. The Federal Reserve has been handed, in effect, a fire hose — and told that the water is also the building."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "This corner is not new. It has been getting deeper since 2009. But in March 2026, with an oil shock hitting, with private credit under stress, and with a new Fed Chair just installed who built his career criticizing the very policies he will now be forced to continue — the corner has never felt smaller."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

We return to the trading floor world for the first time at full intensity. This is a trading floor at the moment of a rate decision announcement — we do not see the specific numbers, only the physical human response. Bodies lean toward screens. Hands move rapidly over keyboards. The light in this world is different from the kitchen table: cold, overhead fluorescent with patches of warm blue-tinted screen glow. The camera moves through the space with the slow, slightly hypnotic drift of a documentary filmmaker who has been given rare access — always in motion, never frantic, 0.5 px/frame plus a slow lateral tracking push. When V2 describes the rate hike cycle, the camera finds a trader's hand moving a mouse — a single controlled, deliberate gesture. When the text arrives at "forty trillion dollars," the camera pulls wide to show the entire floor: dozens of stations, each with multiple screens, each screen showing moving data — the sheer physical scale of the space mirrors the scale of the number. When V2 says "the government would be bankrupt before the cure worked," the camera finds a single trader who has leaned back in their chair and placed both hands behind their head — the universal human gesture of a person confronting a problem without a clean solution. The scene ends on that held image.

### SCENE 4: THE K-SHAPED ECONOMY
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Curiosity | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands how the same economy can look like a boom and a crisis simultaneously — depending on whether you own assets or not

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "But wait. I keep hearing that the economy is booming. GDP growth, stock market near all-time highs, unemployment supposedly low. If things are so bad, why do the numbers look okay?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "This is one of the most important questions a person can ask about economics right now. And the answer is: those numbers are real — but they are measuring a very specific part of the economy. Not the whole economy. Economists call it the K-shaped economy. Imagine a letter K. The upper arm of the K goes up — that represents people who own assets: a house, a stock portfolio, retirement funds, investment accounts. For those people, the last several years have been genuinely excellent. House prices have doubled in many markets. The stock market tripled between 2020 and early 2026. They are spending freely — on travel, on dining, on luxury goods — and because they spend a lot, they are visible in GDP numbers. In the third quarter of 2025, GDP grew at 4.4% annualized. Almost 97% of all new private sector jobs created that year were in health care and social assistance — services consumed disproportionately by older, wealthier Americans. The upper arm of the K is very visible and very noisy. Now look at the lower arm. This represents people who live primarily on wages, who do not own significant assets, who are renters or recent buyers. For those people, the consumer price index has risen 24% since early 2020. Real inflation-adjusted wages for the lowest earners actually declined in the third quarter of 2025 — the first non-pandemic decline since 2014. Total credit card debt hit 1.277 trillion dollars, an all-time record. In the poorest zip codes, one in four credit card balances is ninety or more days past due. University of Michigan consumer sentiment recently dropped to 53, a reading not seen since the Great Recession. The lower arm of the K is quiet, dispersed, and does not show up loudly in GDP. And crucially: it is a much larger group of people."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "When you hear the phrase 'the economy is booming,' it helps to ask: booming for whom? The answer to that question will tell you whether someone is looking at the upper arm or the lower arm of the K. Both are real. Both are the economy. They just happen to different people."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

This is a split-world scene, moving between the two visual worlds explicitly for the first time. We begin at a well-appointed kitchen table with an abundance of food — a proper breakfast being assembled, fresh bread, coffee, oranges being squeezed — all in warm morning light. The camera observes this abundance in close detail: the texture of the bread crust, the glistening of orange juice, the steam from a proper coffee machine. This is the upper arm of the K. Then, on V2's words "the lower arm" — a direct cut. We are at a different kitchen table. Smaller, older. The light is different: cooler, a fluorescent overhead rather than a window. The camera observes with the same close attention: a box of generic cereal, a almost-empty milk carton being shaken to determine if there's enough, a phone on the table showing a mobile banking app — but we cannot see the balance, only that the screen has been opened and stared at. The camera holds here for a long time, longer than feels comfortable, as V2 describes the statistics of the lower arm. The final image before V3 is a close-up of the phone screen, held in a hand — still showing that banking app, still open, still being stared at.

### SCENE 5: TARIFFS — THE PROMISE AND THE MATH
**Duration:** 110 seconds | **Phase:** Curiosity | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands what tariffs are, why they were politically attractive, and what the actual economic data one year later showed

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "So the Trump administration came in promising tariffs would bring manufacturing back, reduce the trade deficit, and eventually replace income taxes. That sounds like a big idea. Did it work?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "Let us follow the actual numbers, which arrived with quiet devastation one year in. The original promise was three-part: tariffs would generate enormous government revenue; they would pressure trading partners to buy more American goods; and they would incentivize companies to build factories in the United States rather than pay the tariffs. After one year, the data told a different story on each count. On revenue: the bipartisan policy tracker showed tariff revenue in the first year was indistinguishable from prior years — the line tracing 2025-to-2026 ran almost exactly along the same path as 2022, 2023, and 2024. ### SCENE 7: GOLD STOPS BEING A COMMODITY
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Curiosity → Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands why gold was behaving differently in 2026 than in any previous cycle — being reclassified as money by central banks, not as a commodity

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "Everywhere I look, people are talking about gold hitting all-time highs. But isn't gold just a shiny metal that doesn't do anything? Why does it matter?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "David Rosenberg — one of the most closely watched economists in North America — said something in early 2026 that crystallized a shift that had been quietly building for years. A commodity — copper, wheat, oil — is priced based on supply and demand for its uses. A monetary asset — a currency, a treasury bond — is priced based on trust in the institutions behind it. What Rosenberg observed was that gold was rising not because jewelry demand had increased or industrial use had surged, but because central banks — the very institutions that issue the currencies gold competes with — were buying gold at the fastest rate in fifty years. By 2025, global central bank gold purchases had been at or near all-time highs for three consecutive years. China, India, Russia, Poland, Hungary, and dozens of smaller nations were all adding gold to their reserves. This is a quiet but extraordinary statement from the institutions that manage the world's official reserves. They are saying: we trust gold more than we trust US government paper. Rick Rule, a legendary resource investor with five decades of experience, put it this way: over the next ten years, he believed the US dollar would lose 75% of its purchasing power — and that gold would in some way mirror that."

### SCENE 8: THE SCOTUS RULING — TARIFFS STRUCK DOWN
**Duration:** 90 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands that the US Supreme Court struck down the tariff regime, why the specific law Trump used was the weak point, and what that meant for market uncertainty

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "Wait, I heard the Supreme Court actually struck down the tariffs. How? And what did that do to the markets?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "This is one of the most revealing episodes of the early 2026 crisis — not because of the ruling itself, but because of the specific law it exposed. To impose sweeping tariffs without Congressional approval, the administration had to use an existing statute. It chose the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 — IEEPA — specifically because, as legal analyst Chris Casey explained, it offered the broadest possible presidential powers of any available law. The others had limitations: on duration, on magnitude, on which countries could be targeted. IEEPA had fewer constraints. But IEEPA was also, as it turned out, the law most vulnerable to the legal argument that imposing permanent tariffs is not an emergency economic power but a trade policy requiring Congressional authorization. The Supreme Court agreed. The tariffs were struck down. Now, you might expect this to produce a clean resolution. Tariffs gone, trade normalized, uncertainty reduced. But the market reaction was more complex. Tariffs going away suddenly meant that companies that had spent a year reorganizing their supply chains, diversifying away from Chinese suppliers, paying premiums for alternative sources — all of that reorganization had costs that were now sunk but no longer strictly necessary. More importantly, the ruling left open the question of what comes next. Would Congress legislate new tariffs? Would the administration find another legal mechanism? The struck-down tariffs had at least been a known quantity. Their absence was a new kind of uncertainty. Markets do not like uncertainty more than they dislike bad news."

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "The SCOTUS ruling is a useful reminder that economic crises do not resolve cleanly. Removing one source of uncertainty almost always reveals two more. The trade war chapter of the polycrisis was not ending. It was changing shape."

### SCENE 10: THE BOND MARKET — THE ULTIMATE REFERENDUM
**Duration:** 105 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands what the bond market is, why rising yields are catastrophic for governments with large debt, and why the bond market is considered the most important market in the world

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "I keep hearing about 'bond yields rising' as if it's a disaster. What is a bond yield and why does everyone care so much about it?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "A bond is simply a loan. When the US government needs to borrow money — which it does constantly, to the tune of trillions of dollars a year — it issues bonds. An investor buys a bond, lends the government money, and in return receives regular interest payments plus the original sum back when the bond matures. The yield is the effective annual return on that bond. Now here is why yield matters above almost everything else. The yield is set not by the government but by the market — by the millions of buyers and sellers who collectively decide what interest rate they demand in exchange for lending to the US government. When they are confident — when they believe the US will repay, that inflation will stay low, that the dollar will hold value — they accept a low yield. When they are worried — when inflation is rising, when debt levels look unsustainable, when geopolitical risk is high — they demand a higher yield. In other words, the bond yield is a real-time, continuously updated vote of confidence in the US government's management of the economy. And here is the catastrophic feedback loop. When yields rise, the government's interest bill rises, because new debt is issued at the higher rate. A government running a large deficit and rolling over large amounts of maturing debt is extraordinarily sensitive to yield changes. In 2025, the US crossed a threshold that had been approaching for years: interest payments on the national debt exceeded the entire defense budget. The bond market had begun sending a message that the stock market had not yet fully processed. Jeff Gundlach — called the Bond King — said in early 2026 that his greatest concern was not inflation, not recession, but a scenario where confidence in US treasury bonds broke down systemically. 'The issue,' he said, 'is not that the dollar might be replaced by something else. It is that the dollar might be replaced by nothing else — a chaotic, fragmented international monetary system.'"

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "The bond market is sometimes called the adult in the room. Stocks can be irrational for years. Bonds tend to catch up with reality faster. In early 2026, the bond market was telling a story that the stock market had not yet entirely believed."

### SCENE 11: PRIVATE CREDIT — THE SHADOW SYSTEM
**Duration:** 130 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner fully understands what private credit is, how it grew from the 2008 crisis's regulatory response, and why it is the hidden fault line in 2026

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "I keep hearing about 'private credit' and 'shadow banking' as if they're connected to everything. What even is private credit and why does it matter so much right now?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "Private credit is any lending that does not involve a regulated bank. That sounds simple, but the implications are enormous. After the 2008 financial crisis, new regulations — the Dodd-Frank Act and its successors — restricted banks from making loans to higher-risk borrowers: small businesses, leveraged buyouts, companies with spotty credit histories. The intent was to make the banking system safer. The result was a void. And into that void stepped a new industry: private credit firms like Apollo Global Management, Blackstone, Blue Owl Capital, and dozens of others. These firms raised money from investors — pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds — and lent it out to the exact borrowers the banks could no longer serve. By early 2026, the private credit industry managed approximately three trillion dollars in assets. Now here is the crucial, haunting detail. That three trillion dollars sounds manageable compared to the US economy's twenty-nine trillion or global public credit markets of over forty trillion. But private credit did not stay contained in its three trillion box. Private credit firms have been buying insurance companies — using insurance premiums, which arrive monthly and take months or years to pay out, as a kind of permanent float to fund additional lending. Apollo bought Athena. Brookfield bought American Equity. KKR took Global Atlantic. The insurance industry manages over forty trillion dollars. And now those forty trillion dollars are partially exposed to a private credit sector that is illiquid, opaque, and under stress. The pension funds that lend to private credit — the funds that hold the retirement savings of teachers, nurses, and public sector workers — are 76% of private credit's investor base. Which means that when private credit firms start having problems, the bag is held not by sophisticated hedge funds with risk managers but by ordinary people's pension accounts."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "Jamie Dimon, the CEO of JPMorgan, said something in early 2026 that became the unofficial summary of private credit risk: 'More cockroaches will emerge.' ### SCENE 14: THE STRAIT OF HORMUZ — THE WORLD'S MOST IMPORTANT WATERWAY
**Duration:** 120 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific geography of the Hormuz Strait, why Qatar's force majeure matters, and what 20% of global LNG supply being suddenly unavailable means for Europe

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "I heard that Qatar's energy company declared something called 'force majeure' and that this almost immediately broke Europe's economy. What actually happened?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "On March 4th, 2026, Qatar Energy — a state-run energy giant that supplies roughly 10 to 15 percent of Europe's entire natural gas — invoked force majeure on all its contracts. In international energy contracts, it is the clause that allows a supplier to halt deliveries without penalty when circumstances beyond their control make delivery impossible. Qatar Energy invoked it because Iranian drone and missile strikes had hit its critical infrastructure at Ras Laffan — the world's largest natural gas export facility — and because the escalating conflict had effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile-wide channel through which Qatar exports 90% of its LNG. Germany was at 30 percent. The Netherlands at an abysmal 10 percent. Europe was entering the spring injection season — the period when storage needs to be refilled for the following winter — with dangerously depleted reserves, and the refill window had just been legally closed. ### SCENE 17: WARFLATION — OIL SHOCK MEETS MONETARY STRESS
**Duration:** 105 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific economic term "stagflation" — and why the combination of oil shock and existing monetary stress creates a scenario the Federal Reserve cannot resolve with standard tools

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "I keep hearing the word stagflation. What is it, and why do economists seem so scared of it?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "Stagflation is one of the rarest and most painful economic conditions a central bank can face. It was first named in the 1970s, when economists discovered to their surprise that two things they had assumed could not coexist — high inflation and high unemployment — were coexisting. The reason they had assumed this was impossible is that the normal economic model said: when unemployment is high, workers have less money to spend, demand falls, and prices fall. But in the 1970s, the opposite happened. Oil prices surged, making everything more expensive — not because consumers were spending too much, but because production itself had become more expensive. This is the difference between demand-pull inflation and supply-push inflation. A central bank can fight demand-pull inflation by raising rates, which reduces spending. It cannot easily fight supply-push inflation, because the inflation is coming from the cost of making things — and raising rates does not make oil cheaper or gas cheaper or shipping cheaper. It just makes borrowing more expensive, slowing the economy without solving the inflation. That is the stagflation trap. You can fight the inflation by raising rates — but that deepens the recession. Or you can fight the recession by cutting rates — but that accelerates the inflation. There is no good lever. In early 2026, Steve Keen, an economist who has studied private debt crises for decades, called this scenario 'warflation.' Not just stagflation, but an oil shock occurring simultaneously with the unwinding of a private debt bubble — a combination he noted had no historical precedent in the post-war era at this scale of debt."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "Dr. Komal Sri-Kumar, who had been predicting stagflation for 2026 before the Iran war even began, said this: 'Do we need to calculate with a wartime economy?' And the answer that most of the analysts we've spoken to gave was: yes. Yes, we do."

### SCENE 18: CHINA'S STRATEGIC PATIENCE — THE OIL STOCKPILE
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Discovery | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands China's specific strategic position in the oil shock — that it had been stockpiling for months, making it relatively insulated, and why this dramatically reshuffled the geopolitical impact

### SCENE 19: THE DOLLAR PARADOX
**Duration:** 95 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands why the dollar simultaneously strengthens in a crisis (because oil is priced in dollars) and weakens long-term (because confidence in US institutions is declining) — the fundamental tension that defines 2026

### SCENE 21: THE HOUSING MARKET — LOCKED AND FROZEN
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific mechanics by which the housing market became simultaneously unaffordable and illiquid — frozen for buyers and sellers alike

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "The housing market seems completely frozen. Nobody can afford to buy and nobody wants to sell. How did it get this bad?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "The housing market froze through the collision of two separate problems. The first is the affordability crisis. At 8% mortgage rates, the monthly payment on a median-priced home requires an income of over $106,000. The median household income is roughly $75,000. Meanwhile, the buyers who can afford to purchase are not young families. ### SCENE 22: THE STOCK MARKET — EVERYTHING IS IN
**Duration:** 115 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific vulnerability of a market where "everybody is all in," why passive investment flows amplify both rises and falls, and what David Rosenberg means by "the big risk is the stock market"

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "The stock market was near all-time highs going into 2026 and then started falling. But isn't that normal? Don't markets go up and down?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "They do. But not all market structures are equally vulnerable to downturns. The specific structure of the stock market in early 2026 created a particular fragility. David Rosenberg — one of North America's most watched economists — said something that encapsulates the risk perfectly: 'Everybody is all in. Everybody is all in. And that has me concerned.' What he meant was this: the passive investing revolution has meant that trillions of dollars flow automatically into equity markets regardless of valuation, through index funds and ETFs. Those flows supported prices through a long bull market. But when the bull market ends — when institutional and retail investors need or want to reduce exposure — those same flows reverse. Every passive fund that needs to sell creates a cascade through every stock in its index. The technical term for this is correlated selling. In a market where everyone owns the same basket of stocks through index funds, everyone sells the same basket of stocks when they need to exit. The sell-off is synchronized in a way that earlier, more diversified market structures were not. Rosenberg added: 'The big risk for the economy is what happens to the stock market. Because if the high-end stop spending — luxury goods, high-end restaurants, cruise lines — if that dries up because the stock market doesn't continue to play ball, this economy is going to show significant strain in the second half of the year.' The upper arm of the K had been spending confidently because its wealth was rising. If the stock market faltered, the upper arm's spending would contract. And the upper arm was now the primary driver of GDP. The stock market was no longer just a financial concern. It was a macroeconomic accelerator."

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "The S&P 500 peaked in late January 2026. By mid-March, it had lost roughly 5% and technicians were watching for the next support levels. ### SCENE 23: BLUE OWL HALTS REDEMPTIONS — THE BANK RUN BEGINS
**Duration:** 120 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific event (Blue Owl redemption halt) that marked the transition from private credit concern to private credit crisis, what a redemption halt means, and the stage-by-stage progression

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "I heard that Blue Owl Capital — one of those private credit firms — actually stopped letting investors take their money out. That seems like something that shouldn't be allowed to happen. How did we get there?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "Jeff Snider of Eurodollar University developed a framework for understanding how private credit crises develop in stages — and it is one of the most useful analytical tools for understanding what happened in early 2026. Stage one: investors start asking questions and withdrawing some capital, but private credit firms can honor the withdrawals using new money coming in plus bank credit lines. This began in September 2025 when two companies — First Brands and Andricolor Holdings — went bankrupt, revealing they had private credit exposure. Stage two: private credit firms run low on cash and begin selling assets — including at discounts — to meet redemptions. This began in February 2026 when Blue Owl Capital, managing $250 billion in assets, ran low on cash and began selling its Buy Now Pay Later loan portfolio at a discount. When a $250 billion firm is forced to sell assets below face value, every other private credit firm that holds similar assets faces a confidence crisis from its own investors. Stage three: forced selling spreads to other firms with different, less-obviously-risky assets — because investors stop differentiating and simply want out of private credit as a category. Blackstone's private credit fund was described as 'on the brink.' Apollo was down 20% in two months. KKR, TPG, and Aries were down up to 35% year-to-date. Stage four — the most feared — is when this crisis spreads beyond private credit into the broader financial system. JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon had warned of exactly this cascade. 'Looking now at the current situation,' ex-Lehman VP Lawrence McDonald said, 'any similarities to 2007? We're in the early stages of a real credit crisis coming out of private credit.'"

[pause]

**V3 (Encouraging Guide):** "The phrase used most frequently about the Blue Owl halt was 'canary in the coal mine.' Mohamed El-Erian used it. Snider used it. ### SCENE 24: BITCOIN — THE ASSET WITH NO SOVEREIGN

### SCENE 25: WEALTH INEQUALITY — THE WIDENING LADDER
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands how QE's asset inflation directly created the wealth gap — and how Kevin Warsh's own analysis of this problem leads to the structural paradox of his appointment

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "Kevin Warsh apparently spent fifteen years saying that the Fed's money printing was the primary cause of wealth inequality. And then he was appointed to run the Fed. Does that make any sense?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "It makes perfect sense as politics and no sense as policy — which is, unfortunately, often how appointments work. Kevin Warsh's analysis was correct and his critique was honest. He pointed to something very specific: the Federal Reserve's QE programs were designed to lower interest rates and raise asset prices. But the people who own assets — stocks, bonds, real estate — are already wealthy. The people who live on wages own very little of these assets. So when asset prices rose dramatically more than the Fed expected, the wealthy became dramatically richer — and the distance between the wealthy and everyone else grew not just in absolute terms but in relative terms. Warsh specifically noted that the cost of climbing the wealth ladder had increased: it now takes more dollars to buy a home, more dollars to buy stocks, more dollars to build a business, while wages had not kept pace with that cost increase. The ladder got taller. The rungs got further apart. And the irony that Heresy Financial's Joe Carson captured perfectly is this: the Federal Reserve's balance sheet must continue to expand, regardless of who is in charge, because the US government's debt service requires it. ### SCENE 27: AI AND THE VELOCITY OF MONEY
**Duration:** 110 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands what the Catrini report argued about AI and jobs, what the report got right and what it missed, and why "velocity of money" is the most important phrase in the debate

### SCENE 29: STAGFLATION CONFIRMED — THE FED'S IMPOSSIBLE CHOICE
**Duration:** 110 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands the specific Fed dilemma in the stagflation context — and why the normal playbook of raising rates to fight inflation makes a weakening labor market and private credit crisis worse simultaneously

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "So the economy is weakening, jobs are being lost, but inflation is also rising because of the oil shock. What does the Fed do? What can it do?"

**V2 (Patient Explainer):** "This is the moment where all the threads we have been following converge in the worst possible way. A central bank has two main jobs: keep prices stable and keep employment high. When only one of these is under threat, the Fed has a clear playbook. When prices are too high, raise rates. When employment is too low, cut rates. But stagflation — prices rising while employment falls — puts these two goals in direct conflict. Raise rates to fight inflation: you make borrowing more expensive, you slow growth further, you cause more layoffs, you deepen the already-weakening labor market. The private credit crisis, which depends on continued rollover of debt at manageable rates, gets dramatically worse. Cut rates to fight unemployment: you add fuel to the already-high inflation, you weaken the dollar, you risk triggering the bond market's worst fears about fiscal irresponsibility. Neither option is acceptable. Both options make something catastrophically worse. This is exactly the scenario that Steve Keen, who has studied private debt crises, called 'warflation' — a word he coined specifically because it needed a new name. As of March 2026, core CPI remained above the Fed's 2% target, but the 'core flexible CPI' — the subset of prices most sensitive to economic activity — was, as David Rosenberg noted, showing 'outright deflation.' Two completely different inflation signals, pointing in opposite directions, existing simultaneously in the same economy. The Fed had to choose which signal to believe and which to ignore. And there was no neutral choice."

### SCENE 32: DE-DOLLARIZATION — THE SLOW-MOTION REVOLUTION
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Synthesis | **Teaching Goal:** The beginner understands what de-dollarization is, how it has actually progressed (more gradual than headlines suggest), and the specific paradox that America's behavior is both causing it and temporarily preventing it

**V1 (Curious Challenger):** "Is the US dollar actually losing its status as the world's reserve currency? Because I keep hearing two opposite things."

### SCENE 34: BANKING STRESS — THE FIRST CRACKS IN THE VISIBLE SYSTEM

### VISUAL WORLDS

**Visual World 1: The Trading Floor**
The physical theater of market decision-making. A real trading floor — rows of screens, keyboards, constantly updating data, human beings moving through concentrated work. This world is chosen because financial markets are where the abstract forces of monetary policy, geopolitics, and debt become instantaneous and physical — price changes in real time, human faces responding to information, the tangible infrastructure of the global financial system. The trading floor is filmed without sensationalism: no shouting, no panic for its own sake. It is a workplace where serious people do serious work in real time. Lighting is dominated by screen glow — a cool blue-white (#BFDBFE) with warm practical lights as counterpoint. The camera's primary mode is slow drift, as though observing without disturbing. Key sequences: Scene 1 (arrival), Scene 22 (market volatility), Scene 38 (polycrisis concept), Scene 41 (synthesis).

**Visual World 2: The Family Kitchen Table**
The intimate domestic counterpart — the place where abstract forces arrive as concrete decisions. A real kitchen table with the accumulated documents, devices, and detritus of a household managing a difficult economic moment: mortgage statements, grocery receipts, phones showing banking apps, sticky notes, double-stickered goods. The kitchen table is not decorated for sadness — it is organized with the quiet care of competent people under real pressure. Lighting is warm but modest: practical household light sources, the kind that illuminate a table where decisions get made after dinner. Key sequences: Scene 4 (K-shaped economy), Scene 12 (national debt becomes personal), Scene 21 (housing), Scene 25 (wealth inequality), Scene 37 (the consumer synthesis), Scene 40 (the window).

**Visual World 3: The Gold Vault**
A specialized sub-world used for precious metals and monetary system scenes. A physical precious metals vault — rows of gold bars, the specific texture of metal under directional lighting, the visual weight of stored value. This world appears five times in the film, each time with slightly different light temperature that reflects the emotional register of the scene: cool and analytical in early appearances; warm and almost ceremonial in later appearances. It grounds the abstract conversation about monetary systems in something physically real. Key sequences: Scene 7, Scene 31, Scene 36, Scene 39.

### VISUAL RULES (consistent across all scenes)

- Constant subtle 0.5 px/frame micro-drift in every shot — the visual world is never completely static
- Extreme macro and close-up photography as the primary visual language for the first half of the film; medium and wide shots earned progressively as understanding accumulates
- Natural, directional lighting throughout — never flat, never clinical; every light source has a direction and a reason
- Shallow to medium depth of field to guide the eye; the blurred background is always a presence, not an absence
- All visuals describe only what a physical camera can capture — no diagrams, no text overlays, no abstract animations, no metaphorical imagery
- Color shifts are gentle and motivated by narration emphasis — the gold vault gets warmer as the film progresses; this is not a filter effect but a lighting choice
- Scene transitions are soft — dissolves and gentle morphs preferred; a hard cut is reserved for genuine shock moments (the Iran war announcement, the Blue Owl halt)
- The two primary visual worlds (trading floor and kitchen table) are never mixed within a single scene until Scene 41, where their convergence constitutes the primary teaching beat
- Every physical object that appears in close-up has been introduced in a wider shot first — the viewer's eye is never surprised by a new texture; it is guided there

## TEACHING SPINE VERIFICATION

The Decompose → Validate → Synthesize pattern is clearly present at both the film-level and the act-level.

**Film-level spine:**

*Decompose (Acts 1–4, Scenes 1–17):* The film introduces the component parts of the polycrisis one at a time, each in its own dedicated teaching space. Scene 2 decomposes the monetary history. Scene 5 decomposes tariff mechanics. Scene 11 decomposes private credit. Scene 13 decomposes the geopolitical background. Each idea is given its own origin beat, mechanism beat, surprise beat, and thesis hook before the next idea is introduced. The beginner is on solid ground at the end of each scene.

*Validate (Acts 5–6, Scenes 18–26):* The middle section of the film returns to the component parts with concrete evidence — specific numbers, specific events, specific consequences. Scene 20 validates the Great Wealth Transfer myth with Federal Reserve data and median inheritance statistics. Scene 22 validates the stock market overextension with passive investing data. Scene 23 validates the private credit stress with the Blue Owl specific event. Scene 26 validates the insurance contagion mechanism with the insurance stock chart patterns. The beginner sees proof that the mechanisms introduced in the Decompose phase are genuinely operating.

*Synthesize (Acts 7–8, Scenes 27–42):* The final section brings the component parts together and shows their interconnections explicitly. Scene 29 synthesizes stagflation's multiple causes. Scene 37 synthesizes the consumer-level impact. Scene 38 synthesizes the polycrisis concept. Scene 41 is the explicit synthesis scene where both visual worlds converge and the complete map is drawn. Scene 42 closes the circle with the gas pump, showing the beginner that they have traveled from anxious incomprehension to structured understanding of the same phenomenon.

**Act-level fractal pattern:**

Each act also contains its own Decompose → Validate → Synthesize movement. For example, Act Four (Iran, Oil, Energy):

This fractal pattern repeats in Acts Two, Three, Five, Six, and Seven, ensuring that even if a viewer tunes in at any point in the film, they enter a local teaching arc that will bring them to a moment of synthesis within five to seven scenes.
