## OVERVIEW

This film begins with a number: $5.6 billion. That is the cost of the first forty-eight hours of the US-Iran War of 2026 — more than NASA's annual budget, spent in the time it takes to drive from New York to Los Angeles and back. The war had been framed as a security operation, a strategic necessity, a message to adversaries. What it also was — what this documentary argues it primarily was — is a financial event of staggering scale, with winners and losers that have almost nothing to do with the map of the Middle East.

The film follows the money. Not as metaphor. As literal journalism: where did $1.5 billion per day actually go? Who had positioned themselves to profit before the first missile flew? How was the other side being financed through the very financial system the US sanctions regime was supposed to police? Who made the political decision to lift Russian oil sanctions in the middle of a war against Russia's ally — and what was the real reason? What does the Supreme Leader's £100 million London property empire, sitting untouched on Billionaire's Row throughout the bombing campaign, tell us about the purpose and limits of economic warfare?

Two visual worlds anchor the film. **THE LEDGER** — clean, cold, institutional spaces where money moves unseen: trading floors at dawn, Bloomberg terminals reflecting in the faces of people who already knew, White House boardrooms with defense CEOs, bank vaults and crypto exchange servers. Cold blue-white light. Steel and glass. The logic of capital. **THE STREET** — where the money's absence is felt in the body: gas station price signs ticking upward on a Tuesday morning, a family at a kitchen table with a calculator, shipping containers piling up at a halted port, an empty pharmaceutical shelf in a hospital. Warm amber fading under harsh fluorescent. The logic of consequence.

The collision between these two worlds is this documentary's argument. Not that war is wrong. That war is expensive in ways that are deliberately invisible — and that visibility is a form of accountability.

### SCENE 1: THE NUMBER
**Duration:** 90 seconds | **Phase:** Hook | **Teaching Goal:** Establish the film's central methodology — every event will be rendered as a financial figure before it is rendered as a human one

#### NARRATION

**V1 (Financial Journalist):** "Five point six billion dollars. That is how much the United States spent in the first forty-eight hours of the Iran War. Not borrowed against. Spent. Ignited. Transformed into kinetic energy and falling buildings and a column of smoke you could see from orbit. Five-point-six billion dollars. To put that in context: that is more than NASA's entire annual budget. It is more than the GDP of Iceland. It is more than the entire annual operating budget of every public school district in the state of Texas combined. In forty-eight hours."

[pause]

**V1:** "By Day Six, the total bill had reached eleven-point-three billion dollars. By Day Twelve: sixteen-point-five billion. The war was running at approximately one-point-five billion dollars per day. Not a budget. A consumption rate. A ledger that added a line every sixty seconds and never subtracted one. And while the missiles were flying, a very specific set of financial instruments was performing extraordinarily well."

[pause]

**V3 (Historian):** "Every war has a price tag. What changes, across centuries, is who gets to see it. In 1914, the British public had no idea what the Somme cost in sterling per day. In 1968, the American public did not receive a daily invoice for Vietnam. In 2026, the numbers exist. They are published. They are in CSIS spreadsheets and Goldman Sachs research notes and CFTC filings. The information is not hidden. What is missing is the argument. Nobody assembled the missile cost alongside the family's gas bill. Nobody put the tanker ETF return beside the small business owner's margin compression. Nobody connected the defense CEO's net worth to the SPR drawdown."

[pause]

**V1:** "This film is that connection. We are going to follow the money. Every dollar. Both directions. Who paid. Who profited. And why those two lists of names almost never overlap."

[pause]

**V2 (Intelligence/Economic Analyst):** "The most useful question in financial journalism is not 'who did this?' It is 'who benefited?' Not who claimed to benefit. Not who was supposed to. Who actually, measurably, in the filings and the portfolio reports and the earnings calls, benefited. The answer, in this war, involves eight weeks of pre-positioning in crude oil futures, a Tether stablecoin pipeline through Binance, a London property empire on Billionaire's Row, and an emergency production mandate signed at the White House on Day Five. We begin with the invoice."

### SCENE 2: THE INVOICE
**Duration:** 130 seconds | **Phase:** Revelation | **Teaching Goal:** Ground viewers in the specific, verified cost figures from CSIS — and make those costs visceral through human-scale comparisons

#### NARRATION

**V2 (Intelligence/Economic Analyst):** "Here is what we know from the Center for Strategic and International Studies war cost analysis, published March thirteenth. Day one and two: five-point-six billion dollars. Three hundred and nineteen Tomahawk cruise missiles. Each one costs three-point-five million dollars. That is three-point-five million dollars of machined steel and guidance systems and solid-fuel propellant, designed to be used exactly once. More than one hundred fifty THAAD interceptors, each one costing between twelve and fifteen million dollars. And Patriot missile systems, depleted by fourteen-point-six percent of total US stockpile. In two days. The United States used fourteen-point-six percent of its Patriot inventory in forty-eight hours. The Patriot production line cannot replace that inventory until April 2027 at minimum."

[pause]

**V1:** "By Day Six, the total war bill had reached eleven-point-three billion dollars. By Day Twelve: sixteen-point-five billion. The war was running at approximately one-point-five billion dollars per day — every single day. To be precise: one billion, five hundred million dollars. Every twenty-four hours. That is roughly the annual education budget of the state of Mississippi, burned through each day. That is not a military budget. That is a destruction rate. And the destruction was not symmetric — because munitions production is slow, and munitions consumption in modern conflict is fast. The gap between those two rates is not just a logistics problem. It is a balance sheet problem."

[pause]

**V2:** "There is a word for the condition in which a military burns through consumable inventory faster than its industrial base can replace it. The word is 'depletion.' And depletion, in a market economy, is an opportunity. Every Tomahawk fired was a Tomahawk that needed to be replaced at emergency-production pricing. Every THAAD interceptor launched was a contract waiting to be written. The war was simultaneously destroying assets and generating the demand for their replacement. The beneficiaries of that demand had been building positions for eight weeks before the first shot."

[pause]

**V3:** "The Roman historian Vegetius wrote, in the fifth century, that 'whoever wants peace must prepare for war.' What he did not calculate — what no war theorist ever quite calculates — is the opportunity cost. Sixteen-point-five billion dollars in twelve days. That is three years of the national school lunch program. That is the entire annual budget of the National Institutes of Health. That is one thousand four hundred elementary schools, built, equipped, staffed for a year. The missiles flew. The schools were not built. That is also a decision — and someone made it."

### SCENE 3: THE HUMAN SCALE
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Revelation → Connection | **Teaching Goal:** Bridge the macro cost figures to the immediate experience of ordinary Americans — gas prices as the daily referendum on war cost

#### NARRATION

**V1:** "While the missiles were flying, something was happening on the ground in America. Gas prices, which had already been rising over the previous weeks, began their next climb. By the time the war entered its second week, the national average was up approximately seventy cents per gallon. That sounds small. That is not small. At the average American driving rate — one thousand miles per month, fuel economy of twenty-five miles per gallon — that is an extra twenty-eight dollars per month per household. Times one hundred and thirty million American households. That is three-point-six billion dollars per month in additional consumer spending on gasoline alone. Nine billion dollars per month. Extracted not by a tax, not by a vote, but by the physics of a conflict eight thousand miles away."

[pause]

**V1:** "Three-point-six billion per month means something specific. It means less money for groceries. For rent. For the medical bill that was already too big. It means the war's cost was already being distributed downward — from the defense budget, through the oil market, to the family with the credit card balance. The inflation transmission mechanism works fast. The political accountability for it works slow."

[pause]

**V2:** "The Strategic Petroleum Reserve release — one hundred seventy-two million barrels, the largest coordinated IEA release in history, across thirty-two countries releasing a combined four hundred million barrels — was designed to blunt this. It worked. Partially. For a while. But every barrel released from the SPR is a barrel that will cost approximately one hundred dollars or more to replace. That is not a solved problem. That is a deferred bill. Deferred bills do not disappear. They compound. The deferred refill cost of the SPR release is approximately twenty billion dollars, payable at a future date, by a future taxpayer, for a decision made in March 2026."

[pause]

**V3:** "There is a concept in public finance called 'intergenerational equity' — the principle that the costs of present decisions should not be borne disproportionately by people who were not present for the decision. The SPR release is a textbook case. The barrel is gone now. The twenty-billion-dollar refill is somebody else's problem. Somebody not yet voting."

[pause]

**V1:** "Gas was at three dollars and seventy-two cents a gallon when the Russia sanctions were lifted. That specific number — three-seventy-two — will matter in Act Four. Hold it."

### SCENE 4: THE DAILY BURN
**Duration:** 120 seconds | **Phase:** Revelation | **Teaching Goal:** Complete the Act 1 economic foundation — the war cost is not abstract, it is structural; the US was burning money at a rate that invites structural economic comparison

#### NARRATION

**V3:** "Let us be precise about what 'one-point-five billion dollars per day' means in historical terms. In 1968, at the height of Vietnam, the war cost approximately twenty-one billion dollars per year — about fifty-eight million dollars per day in then-current dollars. The Iran War of 2026 burned through the equivalent of the entire Vietnam annual war budget in less than two weeks. In twelve days, it spent what the United States spent in an entire year in Southeast Asia. Wars have not gotten cheaper. They have gotten faster."

[pause]

**V2:** "One useful framing: each day of this war cost as much as the entire annual operating budget of the United States Air Force Academy. Each week cost as much as rebuilding a major American city's entire water infrastructure. Each month — had the war lasted a month — would have cost roughly forty-five billion dollars. A month of this war would have eliminated the entire US federal deficit for a quarter. That is an extraordinary compression of economic consequence into a short time window."

[pause]

**V2:** "It also means that the financial instruments positioned before the war would see extraordinary returns in a short time window. A crude oil options position struck at ninety dollars that you bought when oil was at seventy-eight dollars — and held when oil crossed one hundred dollars — does not require months to pay off. It pays off in days. The temporal compression of modern warfare is also the temporal compression of modern war finance. The positions were built slowly. They paid off immediately."

[pause]

**V1:** "Here is the question that drives this entire documentary. Not: was it worth it? That is a political question, and politics will outlast the answer. The real question is: who decided it was affordable? Who was in the room? Who had their financial positions arranged to benefit from the answer being yes? Because somebody knew. And the paper trail — in the CFTC filings, in the tanker ETF returns, in the congressional disclosure forms — says they knew eight weeks in advance. That story begins in Act Two."

[pause]

**V3:** "There is an old saying in markets: 'buy the rumor, sell the news.' In the Iran War of 2026, the rumor was the eight-week positioning. The news was the first missile. The people who bought the rumor made the money. The people who watched the news paid the bill."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The Ledger world: a wide, slow establishing shot of a massive government administrative space — not a war room, a budget room. Long rows of institutional desks in a vast open-plan space, the geometry of American bureaucracy at scale. The camera tracks forward slowly through the rows, at desk level, as if walking toward something at the far end of the room. The desks are largely empty — it is a weekend. Papers are stacked. Coffee cups are cold. A single analyst is working late, hunched over two monitors. The camera passes her slowly. She does not look up. Cut to: an aerial drone shot of an empty freeway overpass at dawn — vast infrastructure, built to last a century, carrying nothing in this moment. Then cut to The Street: a small-town Main Street at dusk — a few storefronts, one CLOSED sign, a woman sweeping in front of a diner, the particular stillness of a town where nothing has happened yet. The war is fourteen hundred miles away. The cost is not.

In normal times, the risk premium for transiting the Strait — the additional insurance cost above the baseline — is a fraction of a percentage point of a vessel's hull value. By mid-March 2026, according to the Insurance Journal's reporting of March seventeenth, maritime insurance premiums for Strait transit had surged more than one thousand percent. The risk premium had become five percent of hull value. Transit cost for a single VLCC supertanker: four million dollars per crossing. The Strait of Hormuz had become the most expensive waterway on earth by a margin that was not close."

[pause]

**V1:** "The cargo rates followed immediately. Asia-to-Europe container rates — the commercial blood supply of the global supply chain, the price signal that determines when goods move from factory to store shelf — jumped nineteen percent. Air freight rates soared as shippers diverted from maritime to airborne transport for time-sensitive cargo. The cost of that diversion passed directly to consumers in the form of higher product prices. Companies that had built just-in-time supply chains assuming stable ocean shipping faced margin compression across every product category: electronics, pharmaceuticals, automotive parts, textiles, industrial components. The war found them in their shipping lanes."

[pause]

**V2:** "The insurance market is, in some ways, the most honest market in the world — because it prices actual risk in real time, with no political motive. A one-thousand-percent surge in Hormuz premiums is the insurance market saying: the probability of losing a ship in this waterway has increased by a factor of ten. That assessment has direct economic consequences. Every vessel that cannot afford the premium cannot transit. Every vessel that can afford it passes the cost to its cargo owners, who pass it to retailers, who pass it to consumers. The four-million-dollar transit premium is distributed across millions of units of goods, invisibly, as a slightly higher price for everything."

[pause]

**V3:** "There is a concept in systems theory called chokepoint amplification — the disproportionate systemic consequence of disrupting a single critical node. The Strait of Hormuz is the world's most consequential chokepoint. A single twenty-one-mile waterway carries one-fifth of global energy. That concentration of consequence in a single geography is not an accident of nature — it is the result of decades of infrastructure investment decisions made in a period of relative stability. The world built the system assuming the Strait would remain open. The war demonstrated the assumption."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The Ledger and The Street simultaneously: a wide aerial view of the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow channel of water between two landmasses, the geometry of a chokepoint made visible from altitude. The camera holds the aerial view, letting the geography speak. Then a push down toward a container ship navigating through the strait, the narrow passage visible in context. Cut to: a shipping insurance underwriting office — multiple screens, analysts studying vessel positions and risk maps (no readable text — just the visual structure of risk assessment, colored zones on a map). One analyst is on a phone, gesturing. The numbers are changing faster than she can price them. Cut to The Street: a supermarket aisle — a stock worker is filling shelves, but the shelves beside her are only partially full. Not empty. Not a crisis. Just... less. The small visible evidence of a disrupted supply chain, three time zones away from the war.

### SCENE 24: GOLDMAN'S CALL
**Duration:** 110 seconds | **Phase:** Reckoning | **Teaching Goal:** Goldman's 25% recession probability plus the pre-war 0.7% GDP revision establishes the economic trajectory — the war hit an economy that was already weakening

#### NARRATION

**V1:** "Goldman Sachs research, published March twelfth, 2026: the bank cuts its US economic outlook. Recession probability over twelve months: twenty-five percent. Up from an already elevated pre-war baseline. The direct mechanism is simple: every ten percent rise in oil prices adds approximately two-tenths of a percentage point to the Consumer Price Index. Oil had risen more than ten percent from pre-war levels. The inflationary addition was already in the pipeline — in the literal sense that the oil was flowing at the new price, and the price was already embedded in supply contracts and futures hedges that would not be renegotiated until they expired."

[pause]

**V2:** "But the pre-war baseline was already fragile, which is the detail that makes the Goldman call serious. GDP in Q4 2025 had been revised down to zero-point-seven percent annualized growth — half the previous estimate of one-point-three percent. The revision came from CNN's reporting on March thirteenth, citing Bureau of Economic Analysis data. Zero-point-seven percent is not a recession, technically. It is not growth that can absorb an external shock. An economy growing at zero-point-seven percent is an economy with no margin. The war provided the shock. The margin was not there."

[pause]

**V1:** "Twenty-five percent probability of recession. That is one in four. That means: the family who absorbed seventy cents more per gallon, who watched their grocery bill rise, who noticed that the credit card balance was not going down this month — they were standing in a one-in-four probability space of living through the beginning of a recession. Not caused by their decisions. Not announced in advance. Not voted on. Transmitted to them through the oil price, which was transmitted from the Strait of Hormuz, which was transmitted from a decision made in a room they never entered, by people whose financial positions were arranged to benefit from the answer being yes."

[pause]

**V3:** "The economist Hyman Minsky observed that stability breeds instability — that the longer an economy is stable, the more leverage accumulates, and the more fragile it becomes to disruption. The Q4 2025 GDP revision suggests an economy that had been leveraged into fragility before the war. The war was the disruption. The Goldman call was the market's assessment that fragility plus disruption equals twenty-five percent probability of the thing that hurts ordinary people most: a recession."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The Ledger world in its analytical register: a research analyst at a desk, late in the afternoon — coffee cup, stack of reports, two monitors. She is writing something, and the writing has a particular quality of deliberateness — this is consequential analysis. Cut to: an abstract visualization of an economic forecast — a line that had been climbing moderately begins to flatten, then angle slightly downward, the shape of a soft landing becoming a harder landing. The camera watches the line's trajectory. Then cut to The Street: a small business — a restaurant, or a dry cleaner, the kind of business that runs on thin margins. The owner is behind the counter, doing mental arithmetic. The costs have changed. The customers haven't — yet. But the calculation has shifted. He looks at the numbers and makes a decision we do not hear.

### SCENE 25: CHINA'S BUFFER
**Duration:** 110 seconds | **Phase:** Global Consequence | **Teaching Goal:** China's 104-day strategic reserve vs. South Korea's 9-day LNG buffer vs. Europe's 30% storage — the war reveals the geopolitical consequences of the SPR decision at the global level

#### NARRATION

**V3:** "While the United States drew down its Strategic Petroleum Reserve to twelve days of consumption, China maintained a different posture. China's strategic petroleum reserve covers one hundred and four days of consumption. One hundred and four days — the equivalent of more than three months of warfare, three months of Strait closure, three months of energy shock that China could absorb without fundamental economic disruption. China is not unaffected by the Hormuz closure. Between forty-five and fifty-seven percent of China's oil imports transit the Strait. But China's one-hundred-and-four-day buffer means it can wait. It can absorb the initial shock. It can negotiate from a position of time, which is the most valuable position in any negotiation."

[pause]

**V2:** "The contrast with other major economies is stark and deliberate. South Korea had nine days of LNG storage when the war began — nine days. Europe was at thirty percent of storage capacity in early March, entering a critical period for LNG replenishment before the following winter. The Hormuz closure's impact on European gas storage calculations was immediate and severe: LNG that should be flowing from Qatar to European terminals was either stuck in the Gulf or was being rerouted at enormous cost premium. The energy security vulnerability that European governments had spent three years trying to reduce after the 2022 gas crisis was being re-exposed by a conflict ten thousand miles away."

[pause]

**V1:** "The knock-on effects reached further than energy markets. Sulfur prices rose sixteen percent in the weeks following the conflict's start — a consequence of reduced sulfur-content crude throughput affecting the feedstocks used in fertilizer production. Fertilizer production is the foundation of global food supply. A sixteen percent rise in sulfur prices means a rise in fertilizer costs. A rise in fertilizer costs, applied at planting season across Asia, means a rise in food production costs. The war's economic radius extended from the Strait of Hormuz to the rice paddies of Asia. In the food inflation data of autumn 2026, the March missile strikes will be visible as a price signal."

[pause]

**V3:** "The asymmetry is structural and it is a policy choice. Countries with strategic reserves — built by deliberate long-term investment, funded by decades of consistent policy — were relatively insulated from the war's energy shock. Countries that had run their reserves down, for quarterly budget reasons, for market efficiency arguments, for the financial logic of just-in-time resource management — were not. This is not fate. It is a choice made in advance of the crisis and collected, with interest, during it."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The Ledger world in a global register: a visual map of the world (no text, purely geographic shape) with several regions highlighted in different colors — intensity of color representing strategic reserve buffer duration. One large region in deep, stable blue (China: 104 days). One region in pale amber (Europe: depleted). One region in warning orange (South Korea: 9 days). The US region shifts from deep blue (pre-war) to pale amber (post-SPR-release). The camera slowly rotates around this global visualization, observing the asymmetries. Cut to: an agricultural vista — a rice paddy or wheat field at planting time, workers in the field, the ordinary labor of food production. The fertilizer prices rising means the cost of this work is about to change. Cut back to the global map: the US region is now amber. China is still deep blue. The camera holds both in frame.

### SCENE 26: THE LEDGER CLOSES
**Duration:** 180 seconds | **Phase:** Closing | **Teaching Goal:** The documentary's synthesis — return to the opening question, answer it, and leave the viewer with the structural implication rather than a verdict

#### NARRATION

**V1:** "So. Who profits when missiles fly?"

[pause]

**V1:** "The hedge funds who spent eight weeks systematically accumulating three hundred and twenty thousand nine hundred and fifty-two Brent crude lots before the first strike — four billion dollars in notional exposure, built in the public CFTC filings, available to anyone who looked. The tanker ETF that gained ninety-eight percent year-to-date on the pre-positioning of a war that was, apparently, predictable from financial markets even if it was not predictable from the news broadcasts. The defense contractors whose stocks hit all-time highs in the first week of conflict — Lockheed Martin up forty percent since June 2025, Northrop Grumman above seven hundred and five dollars, RTX up over one hundred percent in three years — and who left the White House on Day Five with emergency production mandates to quadruple output of the exact weapons that had just been consumed."

[pause]

**V2:** "The Iranian war financiers who moved one-point-seven billion dollars through Binance to the IRGC using a Tether-on-Tron stablecoin architecture that the regulatory system had documented gaps in and had, for reasons both technical and political, not closed. The ghost tanker operators sitting on one hundred and seventy to two hundred million barrels of Iranian crude off the coast of Malaysia — fourteen to seventeen billion dollars in accessible oil revenue, dark, untracked, waiting for the optimal moment. Mojtaba Khamenei, whose one-hundred-million-pound London property empire on Billionaire's Row sat untouched throughout twelve days of Tomahawk strikes against his country's military infrastructure — the missiles did not have a guidance system for The Bishops Avenue."

[pause]

**V3:** "And who pays?"

[pause]

**V1:** "The family at the gas station, absorbing seventy cents more per gallon and three-point-six billion dollars per month in aggregate consumer extraction. The truck driver spreading out three months of fuel receipts, watching the trend line he already knew. The small business owner doing math behind the counter, finding that the numbers no longer add up the same way. The twenty-five percent of Americans living inside Goldman Sachs's recession probability window — one in four, chosen not by their decisions but by the transmission mechanism of a conflict they did not choose. The South Korean households with nine days of LNG. The Asian farming communities absorbing a sixteen percent surge in fertilizer costs from sulfur price pass-through. The future American taxpayers who will refill the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at one hundred dollars per barrel — twenty billion dollars, deferred, accruing."

[pause]

**V3:** "Every war is, in part, a balance sheet. The balance sheet of the Iran War of 2026 — incomplete, still being written, many of its entries still classified — already has one consistent feature. The people in the profit column are not the people who went to war. The people who went to war are in the cost column. This observation is not new. What is new is the documentation. The numbers are in the CFTC filings. In the CSIS spreadsheets. In the Bloomberg property records. In the Chainalysis crypto crime report. In the Goldman Sachs research note. In the Insurance Journal's Hormuz premium data. In the Navnoor Bawa analysis of pre-war positioning. The ledger is public. The frame was missing."

[pause]

**V2:** "The frame matters because the frame determines the question. Without it, the question is: who won the war? With it, the question is: who won from the war? These are different questions with different answers. The first is answered by military historians. The second is answered by following the money. We followed it for sixty minutes. The trail was not hard to find. It was in the public record. Waiting for someone to read it in the same order."

[pause]

**V1:** "This was the frame."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The documentary's visual argument assembled in its final sequence. Begin with a slow, meditative aerial view of open ocean — the Strait of Hormuz from very high altitude, the chokepoint geography visible as pure abstract geometry. The water is blue-grey. The land on either side is brown-tan. The passage between them is the whole story. The camera holds this overhead view, slowly drifting. Then a sequence of quick-cut returns to the film's key images — not linear, but associative: the trading floor at dawn, the woman at the gas pump, the Bishops Avenue gate, the CEO entering the White House, the underground SPR cavern, the ghost tanker silhouette, the gas pump receipt on the empty conference table, the empty padlock on the surface. Not a recap. A closing argument in images. Then the final shot: The Street in its most human register. A long shot down an ordinary city block at dusk — a neighborhood, people walking home, a store closing, a child on a bicycle. The ordinary, irreducible texture of life that continues after every war, during every budget decision, at every gas station. The camera slowly pulls back until the block is small in the frame, and the frame is mostly sky. Hold. Fade to black.
