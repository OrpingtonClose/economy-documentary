## OVERVIEW

This film takes a shaken, news-exhausted viewer on a fifty-five-minute reckoning with a war that did not have to happen. We begin with a sound — a heartbeat in darkness — and the documentary never lets that heartbeat slow to rest. The argument is not that the United States and Iran were destined for peace. The argument is something harder and more precise: that the specific war that began on February 28, 2026, was the product of choices — choices made by identifiable people, in identifiable rooms, with identifiable information they chose to ignore. From the strike on Khamenei's convoy, through the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the cascade of economic and human catastrophe that followed, to the moment a single counterterrorism official stood up and resigned rather than be complicit — this documentary traces the architecture of a disaster. It does not traffic in propaganda from either side. It asks the question that every war demands: was this necessary? And it is honest enough to answer. Two visual worlds anchor the film throughout. The first is **The War Room** — the interior spaces of military and political power where the decisions were made: Pentagon briefing rooms washed in cool fluorescent light, White House Situation Room conversations that leaked into history, UN Security Council chambers where alliances fractured in real time. These spaces are precise, hierarchical, and lit with the cold logic of institutions. The second is **The Street** — the unmediated human consequence: Iranian neighborhoods where families were eating dinner when the missiles came, American military families watching their phones at 3 a.m., protest marches from Tehran to Berlin to Los Angeles, refugee camps blooming along Lebanon's southern coast. These two worlds exist in permanent collision throughout the film, and their collision is the film's central argument: that the distance between the War Room and The Street is not a feature of modern warfare — it is its deepest pathology.

### SCENE 1: THE LAST NORMAL NIGHT
**Duration:** 90 seconds | **Phase:** Wonder | **Teaching Goal:** Establish the world before the strike — the ordinary texture of the days that preceded an extraordinary rupture — so the viewer feels what was lost

#### NARRATION

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "February 27, 2026. A Friday night in Tehran. People are eating dinner. Children are doing homework. The city hum — the ordinary, irreducible hum of ten million people going about their lives — is continuous, unbroken. Nobody in that city knows that in Washington, in a room with no windows, a decision has already been made."

[pause]

**V2 (Intelligence Analyst):** "In diplomatic terms, the weeks before Operation Epic Fury were not peaceful — but they were managed. Back-channel communications between Tehran and Washington had been ongoing for months. There were no imminent military provocations documented in the intelligence record. The US counterterrorism apparatus had been watching Iran closely, and what they saw was a regime under economic pressure, politically cautious, not on an offensive footing. That assessment existed in writing. It would later become the document at the center of the most important resignation of 2026."

[pause]

**V3 (Historian):** "Every war has a last normal night. The last night before Dresden. The last night before the Gulf of Tonkin. They all look the same from the inside — unremarkable, a little tired, full of small plans for the next day. It is only in retrospect that we see what they were: the last moment before a choice that could not be unmade."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The camera drifts with a barely perceptible handheld float, close enough to feel human warmth but distant enough to hold the whole neighborhood. A child runs across the frame below, pursued by another child. A man on a scooter navigates a narrow alley. The light is warm — the copper-gold of a city before modern LED, the kind of light that exists everywhere people have lived for centuries. As V2 begins, the camera very slowly descends further, passing through layers of the neighborhood — a café's open window releasing music, a television's blue flicker behind a curtain — until it settles at street level, watching the ordinary foot traffic of a Friday evening. As V3 speaks of the "last normal night," the camera lingers on a single still figure — an old man sitting on a plastic chair outside a shop door, drinking tea, watching nothing in particular, absolutely at rest in a world that is, for the moment, at peace.

### SCENE 3: THE ROOM WHERE IT WAS DECIDED
**Duration:** 100 seconds | **Phase:** Tension | **Teaching Goal:** The viewer understands that Operation Epic Fury was not a spontaneous response to provocation — it was a planned operation, greenlit in a specific room, over specific objections

**V2 (Intelligence Analyst):** "The decision to kill Khamenei did not arrive suddenly. It was prepared. The operation had a name — Epic Fury — which tells you something about the cultural register of the people who named it. What it did not have, according to multiple officials who participated in the planning process, was a serious second question: what happens the morning after?"

[pause]

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "Reporting on that planning process, and on the internal dissent, emerged in the days following the strike. We know that intelligence officials raised concerns. Those objections were overruled. The operation proceeded. Fifty thousand American troops were already in the region. In Washington, no formal declaration of war was sought or issued."

[pause]

### SCENE 5: IRAN ANSWERS
**Duration:** 90 seconds | **Phase:** Tension | **Teaching Goal:** Iran's immediate response — the Strait of Hormuz closure and the first retaliation — was not a surprise to anyone who had been paying attention

#### NARRATION

**V3 (Historian):** "Every action in this conflict was, in retrospect, predictable. Not certain — but predictable. The American intelligence community had models for Iranian behavior under extreme provocation. Assassination of the Supreme Leader was the most extreme possible provocation. The models said: the Strait of Hormuz would close. The models were right."

[pause]

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "Within hours of the strike, the IRGC had deployed vessels to the strait. Shipping traffic through the world's second-busiest commercial waterway — forty to sixty tankers a day — slowed, then stopped. Iran's new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, had yet to appear publicly. He was still in medical care, injured in the same strike that killed his father. But his first statement, read on Iranian state television by a news anchor with barely controlled trembling, was direct: the Strait of Hormuz would remain closed. The world's oil supply would be at war."

[pause]

**V2 (Intelligence Analyst):** "The Strait of Hormuz is twenty-one miles wide at its narrowest point. Through that twenty-one miles passes twenty percent of the world's daily oil supply. One fifth of all liquefied natural gas. Essentially all of the energy that powers South Korea, Japan, Taiwan — nations with no energy reserves of their own, who are also US allies. Closing the strait did not hurt Iran. It hurt everyone else. That asymmetry — Iran with little to lose, the world with everything to lose — was always the central strategic calculation. The question is whether anyone in the War Room was doing that math."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

A wide, cool-toned aerial shot of the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow blue-grey water between two landmasses, dotted with tiny vessel shapes. The camera tracks slowly along the strait's length, establishing its geography. Then a rapid sequence: a patrol boat turning hard, white wake cutting the grey water; a larger vessel at anchor, military escort closing in; shipping lane AIS tracking data aesthetic (no text/numbers, but the pattern of ships suddenly absent where they had been dense). As V1 speaks of the closure, the visual rhythm slows — a single supertanker at anchor, idle, not moving. The shot lingers on that stillness: a vessel designed to be in perpetual motion, frozen. As V2 makes the case for the math that was not done, the camera finds an aerial view of the 21-mile gap from above — the water between two dark landmasses — and holds on it, letting the scale of the bottleneck register.

### SCENE 18: THE HEARING

### SCENE 21: #NOTOURWAR

### SCENE 30: THE WORLD AFTER
**Duration:** 95 seconds | **Phase:** Reflection | **Teaching Goal:** The long-term strategic landscape post-war — Iran's potential political trajectory, the Middle East's destabilization, the fracturing of the old order

**V3 (Historian):** "What comes after is not clear. What is clear is that clarity itself has been damaged. The Islamic Republic of Iran was, as of mid-March 2026, still governing. The new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, had not been seen publicly since the opening strikes, but was issuing statements through state media, appointing officials, maintaining the machinery of a state that was simultaneously under military attack and in political succession. The regime had not collapsed. The population had not risen to replace it. The war had not produced the outcome the administration had imagined."

[pause]

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "What the war had produced, with certainty, was: a new Supreme Leader who was the son of the one who was killed — and who was harder, more committed to retaliation, and had no interest in any deal that began with American terms. ### SCENE 31: THE G20 EMERGENCY

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "The G20 emergency summit convened within weeks of the war's start. [pause]

**V2 (Intelligence Analyst):** "The diplomatic geometry of the G20 in 2026 was complicated. What they do produce, always, is a record: the world was here, the world saw this, the world tried to respond. ### SCENE 32: THE CHOICE
**Duration:** 120 seconds | **Phase:** Reflection | **Teaching Goal:** The closing scene — delivering the documentary's thesis and closing message; the veteran on the street; the question of whether this was worth it

#### NARRATION

**V3 (Historian):** "The US-Iran War of 2026 was not an accident of history. It was a choice. A choice made in the shadows, without public debate, without clear objectives, and without a plan for what came next. This war was sold as a necessary strike against a dangerous enemy. But it was built on a foundation of fear, misinformation, and political expediency. Every catastrophe contains within it the record of the choices that produced it. Those choices were made by people who had access to better information and chose not to use it. By people who were warned, in writing, and overruled the warning. By people who, when asked the second question — what happens next? — had no answer."

[pause]

**V1 (War Correspondent):** "The human beings who paid the costs of those choices were not, in most cases, the people who made them. Sergeant Nola Moore was not in the White House when the decision was made. Sergeant Declan Cody did not vote for the operation. The thirteen hundred Iranian civilians killed in the strikes did not have a representative in any of the planning meetings. The three hundred thousand Lebanese forced to flee were not consulted. This is always true of war. It is always worth saying out loud."

[pause]

**V2 (Intelligence Analyst):** "The question before us is not whether the war of 2026 can be undone. It cannot. The question is whether the architecture that produced it — the concentrated executive power, the suppressed dissent, the absence of congressional authorization, the dismissal of intelligence, the lack of contingency planning — has been examined, exposed, and addressed in ways that make the next catastrophic choice less likely. The answer, as of March 2026, is: not yet."

[pause]

**V3 (Historian):** "The question we must ask ourselves now is not how it happened. We know how it happened. We have told you. The question is whether we have the courage to ensure it never happens again. The next war is always closer than we think. And it will begin, as this one did, in a room without windows, with a decision that has no plan for what comes next."

#### VISUAL DESCRIPTION

The closing visual sequence is a deliberate, unhurried journey through the two worlds of the film, now merged. It begins on an American street — not dramatic, not desolate, but quiet: a man sitting on a curb outside a VA building or a government services office. He is middle-aged, wearing civilian clothes, and he has the specific posture of someone who has been in a place that was not a good place and is now sitting in this ordinary, unremarkable street and is not sure what to do with himself. The camera approaches him slowly — he does not look at the camera; he is looking at nothing in particular, or at the middle distance, which is where people look when they are thinking about something they cannot resolve. As V3 delivers the thesis, the camera cuts back and forth between this man on the curb and a series of faces we have seen throughout the film — or faces like them: the woman in Tehran with hands around a coffee mug, the faces in the protest crowd, the delegate whose chair was empty, the reader of reports. The documentary's living cast of the people who paid the costs. As V1 speaks the names of Sergeant Moore and Sergeant Cody, the camera holds on the veteran's face. His eyes do not move. He is carrying something. As V2 speaks of "not yet," the camera begins to pull back from the veteran, slowly, in the sustained backward pull that echoes Scene 1's descending crane. As V3 delivers the final closing lines — "the next war is always closer than we think" — the camera has pulled back far enough that the veteran is a small figure on a curb, in a large street, in a large city, in a large world that has not yet learned what it needed to learn. The light is the gold of late afternoon — the last light before dark. The camera holds on this image as the narration ends, then continues to hold for several seconds more, letting the silence do its work, before a slow fade to black. The heartbeat from Scene 2 returns — slow, alone in the darkness — and then it, too, fades.
