# The History of Coffee

## Origins

Coffee was first discovered in Ethiopia around the 9th century. Legend has it that a goat herder named Kaldi noticed his goats became energetic after eating berries from a certain tree. The berries were from the coffee plant, Coffea arabica.

The practice of roasting and brewing coffee beans spread from Ethiopia to Yemen in the 15th century. Sufi monks used coffee to stay awake during long prayer sessions. By the 16th century, coffee had spread throughout the Middle East, Persia, Turkey, and North Africa.

## The Coffee Trade

The global coffee trade is worth over $100 billion annually. Brazil is the world's largest producer, followed by Vietnam, Colombia, and Indonesia. Coffee is the second most traded commodity in the world after crude oil.

A single coffee plant takes 3-5 years to produce its first harvest. Each plant yields about 2,000 coffee cherries per year, which translates to roughly 1 pound of roasted coffee. The average American drinks 3.1 cups of coffee per day.

## Processing Methods

There are three main processing methods:

1. **Washed (Wet) Process**: The fruit is removed before drying. This produces a cleaner, brighter cup with higher acidity. Most specialty coffees use this method.

2. **Natural (Dry) Process**: The whole cherry is dried with the fruit still on the bean. This creates a sweeter, fruitier flavor profile. Common in Ethiopia and Brazil.

3. **Honey Process**: A hybrid where some fruit pulp is left on during drying. The name comes from the sticky, honey-like texture of the beans during processing.

## Health Effects

Research from the New England Journal of Medicine (2012) found that coffee drinkers had a lower risk of death from heart disease, respiratory disease, stroke, injuries, accidents, diabetes, and infections. The study followed 400,000 people over 13 years.

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the brain, which prevents drowsiness. A typical cup contains 95mg of caffeine. The half-life of caffeine is 5-6 hours, meaning half the caffeine from your afternoon coffee is still in your system at bedtime.

Coffee contains over 1,000 chemical compounds, including chlorogenic acids (antioxidants), trigonelline (gives coffee its aroma), and cafestol (raises cholesterol but may protect the liver).

## Economic Impact

Coffee farming employs over 125 million people worldwide. In many developing countries, coffee is the primary source of foreign exchange. Ethiopia alone has 15 million people dependent on coffee production.

The "coffee crisis" of 2001-2004 saw prices drop below $0.50 per pound, devastating farmers. Fair trade certification emerged partly in response, guaranteeing minimum prices. Today, fair trade coffee accounts for about 5% of global sales.

Climate change threatens coffee production. A 2014 study predicted that by 2050, the area suitable for growing coffee could be reduced by up to 50%. Rising temperatures increase pest pressure from the coffee berry borer and leaf rust fungus.
